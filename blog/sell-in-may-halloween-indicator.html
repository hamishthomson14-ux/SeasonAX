    The Halloween Indicator: Does "Sell in May" Work?

    TimingAX Research  ·  May 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  S&P 500, global markets

    The most famous phrase in investing, tested

    "Sell in May and go away, come back on St Leger's Day." It's the most quoted piece of market folklore in history. It's also, in rough outline, supported by a well-known academic study and by our own 15-year figures. The more interesting question is what exactly the data shows, and what it doesn't.

    The academic version of the same observation is called the Halloween Indicator, named for the switching date of October 31. Documented in the American Economic Review in 2002 by Bouman and Jacobsen, it found that equity markets in 36 of 37 countries showed meaningfully higher returns in the November–April period than the May–October period. Not just the US. Not just recently. 36 of 37 countries.

    +1.18%S&P 500 average monthly return, November–April  ·  vs +0.90% for May–October

    The numbers: what 15 years shows

    Over the last 15 years, the S&P 500's average monthly return in the November–April window was +1.18%, against +0.90% for May–October. In the US the gap has narrowed in recent years — both halves have been positive on average — but the winter months have still done more of the heavy lifting, and the pattern is stronger in several other markets.

    "36 of 37 countries showed higher returns November–April. This is not a US anomaly."

    Why does the winter period outperform?

    The honest answer is that nobody has definitively proven the mechanism. The main competing theories are:

    • Holiday-spending optimism. The idea is that optimism around the holiday season lifts consumer and retail stocks, and with them the broader indices.
    • Institutional year-end positioning. The idea is that fund managers build positions for the following year in November and December, and that January rebalancing adds support.
    • Reduced summer risk appetite. The idea is that lighter trading over the summer means lower liquidity, so news moves prices more, and that this tends to weigh on markets.
    • Earnings cycle timing. The idea is that third-quarter results, reported in October and November, set the tone for the year-end rally.

    What the strategy doesn't do

    Here's what the slogan obscures: the May–October period isn't reliably negative. It's just less reliably positive. The S&P 500's average monthly return in those months is +0.90%, not negative. From the end of April to the end of October 2020, during the COVID recovery, the S&P 500 rose 12.28%. Over the same stretch in 2021 it rose 10.13%, despite a −4.76% September. The seasonal tendency is a historical average, not a rule.

    The practical version of the strategy isn't binary switching between stocks and cash. Most seasonal investors use it to tilt: overweight equities in the winter months, reduce exposure or move toward defensive sectors in summer, without fully exiting. That approach captures most of the seasonal edge without the whipsaw risk of missing a strong summer.

    +10.13%S&P 500, end of April to end of October 2021  ·  a “weak” half that rose

    The TimingAX view

    The Halloween Indicator is the original seasonal pattern — everything else on the TimingAX platform is a refinement of this basic observation applied to specific assets and time windows. For the S&P 500, November is the strongest individual month (+3.11% average, 87% win rate). September is the weakest (−1.24%, 44% win rate). That monthly spread is the mechanical expression of the seasonal effect at the individual month level.
