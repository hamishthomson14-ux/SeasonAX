    Why September Is the
    S&P 500’s Weakest Month

    TimingAX Research  ·  June 2026  ·  8 min read  ·  S&P 500, NASDAQ, global indices

    The most consistent pattern in US equities

    If you have to choose one seasonal pattern to remember, it's this: September has been the weakest month. Not in every year — but on average, over the S&P 500’s last 15 years, and in the UK and Germany too.

    The S&P 500 averages −1.24% in September over 15 years — the only month to average a net loss. The NASDAQ Composite is similar at −1.18%. The win rate — the percentage of Septembers where the market ends the month higher than it started — is just 44% for the S&P and 50% for the NASDAQ. Historically, September has been a coin-flip at best.

    −1.24%
    S&P 500 average September return  ·  15-year period  ·  44% win rate

    It is a tendency, not a rule: the S&P 500 still rose in 44% of the last 15 Septembers. But across that period, no other month has averaged a loss.

    "Over the last 15 years, September is the only month in which the S&P 500 has averaged a net loss."

    Why might it happen? Possible explanations

    Nobody has proven why September has been weak. These are the explanations most often given — theories, not established facts.

    1. The mutual-fund tax calendar

    Under US tax rules (Internal Revenue Code §4982), mutual funds measure capital gains for their required annual distribution over the 12 months to 31 October. Some argue this encourages funds to sell losing positions in September and October, before that cut-off.

    2. The end of summer

    Trading volumes are typically lower in August. One argument is that when portfolio managers return in September, they cut risk that built up over the summer, adding to selling.

    3. A busy event calendar

    The Federal Reserve holds eight scheduled policy meetings a year, and one usually falls in September; the month also leads into third-quarter earnings. Some argue that this concentration of events makes investors more cautious.

    The global version of the same pattern

    What makes September seasonality particularly credible is that it isn't a US anomaly. The FTSE 100 averages −0.74% in September. The DAX averages −0.53%. Three major markets in three different countries, with different regulatory environments, different investor bases and different fiscal years, producing the same result. Japan is the notable exception: the Nikkei has averaged +0.81% in September, a reminder that seasonal patterns are market-specific and worth checking asset by asset.

    The data in practice: worst September cases

    The average masks some genuinely severe individual Septembers. September 2022 delivered -9.3% for the S&P 500 — its worst month of 2022, a year in which the Federal Reserve raised interest rates seven times. September 2001 saw the post-9/11 reopening plunge. September 2008 had the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy. September 1998 had the LTCM crisis.

    -9.3%
    S&P 500 September 2022  ·  its worst month of 2022

    How to read this

    Seasonal patterns describe the past; they are not predictions, and this is not investment advice. A few points keep the September figures in proportion:

    • Many Septembers were positive. The S&P 500 rose in 44% of them over the last 15 years.
    • It varies by market. The Nikkei 225 has averaged +0.81% in September. Check the asset you care about.
    • Check the track record. Our track record shows how often seasonal signals have actually been right, month by month, out of sample.
    • Costs and taxes matter. Any strategy built on a monthly average has to overcome trading costs and taxes, which these figures don’t include.

    The Halloween Indicator:
    Does "Sell in May" Work?

    TimingAX Research  ·  May 2026  ·  10 min read  ·  S&P 500, global markets

    The most famous phrase in investing, tested

    "Sell in May and go away, come back on St Leger's Day." It's the most quoted piece of market folklore in history. It's also, in rough outline, supported by a well-known academic study and by our own 15-year figures. The more interesting question is what exactly the data shows, and what it doesn't.

    The academic version of the same observation is called the Halloween Indicator, named for the switching date of October 31. Documented in the American Economic Review in 2002 by Bouman and Jacobsen, it found that equity markets in 36 of 37 countries showed meaningfully higher returns in the November–April period than the May–October period. Not just the US. Not just recently. 36 of 37 countries.

    +1.18%
    S&P 500 average monthly return, November–April  ·  vs +0.90% for May–October

    The numbers: what 15 years shows

    Over the last 15 years, the S&P 500's average monthly return in the November–April window was +1.18%, against +0.90% for May–October. In the US the gap has narrowed in recent years — both halves have been positive on average — but the winter months have still done more of the heavy lifting, and the pattern is stronger in several other markets.

    "36 of 37 countries showed higher returns November–April. This is not a US anomaly."

    Why does the winter period outperform?

    The honest answer is that nobody has definitively proven the mechanism. The main competing theories are:

    • Holiday-spending optimism. The idea is that optimism around the holiday season lifts consumer and retail stocks, and with them the broader indices.
    • Institutional year-end positioning. The idea is that fund managers build positions for the following year in November and December, and that January rebalancing adds support.
    • Reduced summer risk appetite. The idea is that lighter trading over the summer means lower liquidity, so news moves prices more, and that this tends to weigh on markets.
    • Earnings cycle timing. The idea is that third-quarter results, reported in October and November, set the tone for the year-end rally.

    What the strategy doesn't do

    Here's what the slogan obscures: the May–October period isn't reliably negative. It's just less reliably positive. The S&P 500's average monthly return in those months is +0.90%, not negative. From the end of April to the end of October 2020, during the COVID recovery, the S&P 500 rose 12.28%. Over the same stretch in 2021 it rose 10.13%, despite a −4.76% September. The seasonal tendency is a historical average, not a rule.

    The practical version of the strategy isn't binary switching between stocks and cash. Most seasonal investors use it to tilt: overweight equities in the winter months, reduce exposure or move toward defensive sectors in summer, without fully exiting. That approach captures most of the seasonal edge without the whipsaw risk of missing a strong summer.

    +10.13%
    S&P 500, end of April to end of October 2021  ·  a “weak” half that rose

    The TimingAX view

    The Halloween Indicator is the original seasonal pattern — everything else on the TimingAX platform is a refinement of this basic observation applied to specific assets and time windows. For the S&P 500, November is the strongest individual month (+3.11% average, 87% win rate). September is the weakest (−1.24%, 44% win rate). That monthly spread is the mechanical expression of the seasonal effect at the individual month level.

    Uptober: What the Data Shows
    About Bitcoin in October

    TimingAX Research  ·  April 2026  ·  7 min read  ·  Bitcoin, crypto seasonality

    Bitcoin’s strongest month

    Bitcoin averages +15.16% in October across 13 years of data — its strongest month of the year. It has been positive in 9 of 13 Octobers, a 69% win rate. In an asset class defined by volatility and chaos, that level of calendar consistency is remarkable enough to warrant serious analysis.

    +15.16%
    Bitcoin average October return  ·  13 Octobers  ·  69% win rate

    "Uptober" has been part of crypto trading culture for years, and here the data backs up the nickname: October has been Bitcoin's strongest month on average. Why is less clear — and a strong average is not a guarantee: our track record shows how often Bitcoin's seasonal signals have actually been right.

    Three explanations people give

    1. Halving cycle timing

    Bitcoin's halvings (which cut the issuance rate by 50%) have occurred in November 2012, July 2016, May 2020, and April 2024. In each of the last three cycles, Bitcoin’s closing high came about a year and a half after the halving: 525 days after the 2016 halving (December 2017), 546 days after the 2020 halving (November 2021) and 534 days after the April 2024 halving, on 6 October 2025 ($124,753). Whether this cycle explains October strength specifically is debated.

    2. Institutional positioning

    One theory is that, since Bitcoin futures launched in December 2017 and institutional crypto markets grew, more professional money positions ahead of the fourth quarter, as in traditional markets. We haven’t found data that isolates this effect.

    3. Recovery from September weakness

    Bitcoin's September is one of its weakest months: averaging −1.14% with a 50% win rate, before October's +15.16%. One argument is that sellers are exhausted after September and buyers step in as the calendar turns, and that, as more people expect a strong October, buying ahead of it adds to the effect. Both are hard to test.

    "September is one of Bitcoin's weakest months at −1.14%. October is its best at +15.16%."

    The three bad Octobers

    Bitcoin's negative completed Octobers in our data are 2014, 2018 and 2025. The first two occurred during sustained bear markets where macro and structural forces overwhelmed seasonal tendencies. The 2018 case is instructive — October 2018 was negative despite strong seasonals, as the broader crypto bear market (in which Bitcoin fell 83% from its December 2017 closing high to its December 2018 closing low) dominated every other factor.

    This is the key qualifier: seasonal patterns are tendencies that hold when other forces are neutral or aligned. When a bear market is in full force, no seasonal pattern reliably reverses it. The 69% win rate already prices in those failures — years where the backdrop was too negative for seasonality to overcome.

    Beyond Bitcoin: the crypto seasonal calendar

    Ethereum, by comparison, averages +4.81% in October. Bitcoin has averaged +7.51% in November and +7.14% in December.

    Bitcoin's weakest months have been January (−1.15%) and September (−1.14%).
